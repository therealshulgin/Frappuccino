# Key management & on-device secrets — Design rationale (adversarial steelman)

> Model: claude-opus-4-8[1m]. Date: 2026-06-28. Scope: how Frappuccino derives, holds, seals, and wipes the witness's key material on a phone facing a state adversary (seizure, Cellebrite-class forensics, coercion).

This is a design-defense document, not a vulnerability hunt. For each choice it makes the affirmative case, justifies the specific implementation against the alternatives, then states the sharpest hostile objection and either rebuts it or concedes it as a known residual. Every claim is grounded in the code with `file:line` citations.

The motto governs everything here: *"tout quitte le téléphone & une saisie n'expose rien ; le téléphone est un relais, pas un coffre."* The entire key-management design is the concrete realization of the second clause — **the phone holds no long-term vault**. What it holds is (a) public material, (b) a few seed-derived secrets sealed under a PIN and re-derivable from the paper phrase, and (c) an ephemeral ratchet whose forward secrecy means a live extraction forges nothing in the past.

---

## 1. Scope & the choices under review

- **Sovereign 12-word BIP-39 key, generated on-device from OS entropy, never stored** — `crypto-rs/core/src/bip39.rs:198-247` (`from_entropy`, `generate_fr`, `fresh_entropy`); held only in RAM during onboarding, `mobile/.../onboarding/MnemonicHolder.kt:28-78`.

- **V2 lifecycle asymmetry: `StreamIdentity` (public, storable) vs `EnrollmentKit` (one-shot long-term Ed25519, wiped after enrollment) vs `ArchiveIdentity` (X25519, re-derived on demand)** — `crypto-rs/core/src/identity.rs:66-159, 176-332, 338-428`.

- **Auto-lock that wipes the ratchet + PIN-session at idle, never during recording/drain** — `mobile/.../util/V2LockTimeoutController.kt:51-234`; `lock()` at `StreamUploadManager.kt:332-354`.

## 2. Choice-by-choice rationale

### 2.1 Sovereign phrase-derived key, never stored (vs a stored keystore-wrapped key)

- **What it is.** A 12-word, 128-bit BIP-39 key is generated on-device from the OS CSPRNG (`bip39.rs:231-234` `fresh_entropy` → `rand_core::OsRng`; `bip39.rs:241-247` `generate_fr`), shown to the user once during onboarding, and **never written to disk**. Every secret is derived from it: BIP-39 PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA512, 2048 iterations, → 64-byte seed (`bip39.rs:167-190`), then per-role HKDF (`identity.rs:198-245`). The seed type is zero-on-drop with redacted `Debug` (`bip39.rs:54-82`).

- **Affirmative case.** This is the load-bearing realization of "the phone is a relay, not a vault." Against a state adversary who *will* eventually get the device, the most valuable asset is the root key. If it is not on the device, no amount of forensic extraction, no Cellebrite chip-off, no coerced unlock can recover the witness's archive-decryption capability *from the phone alone*. Sovereignty is also recovery: a destroyed/seized phone does not destroy the archive — the witness re-derives `ArchiveIdentity` and the report addresses on a fresh device from the paper phrase (`identity.rs:349-386`, `report.rs:172-202`). The key belongs to the human, not the hardware.

- **Why this implementation.** Alternatives considered and rejected:
  - *Android Keystore / StrongBox / TEE-wrapped key.* Rejected because it binds the key to the seized hardware. A coerced unlock (the adversary in this model can compel a PIN) hands over a TEE that will happily decrypt. It also leaves a recoverable secret on the device — the opposite of the motto. And it is unportable: lose the phone, lose the archive.
  - *Random 32-byte key escrowed to the server.* Rejected outright — the relay is explicitly untrusted ("relais, pas un coffre"); escrow recreates the vault off-device.
  - *Higher-entropy phrase (24 words / 256 bits).* 12 words / 128 bits is the deliberate usability/security balance: 128 bits is far beyond brute-force, and a witness under stress must transcribe the phrase to paper accurately. The FR wordlist is embedded verbatim and asserted to be exactly 2048 words at load (`bip39.rs:306-325`) so the derivation is byte-exact with the Kotlin reference.
  - BIP-39 specifically (not a bespoke scheme) gives a human-checkable checksum, an accent-tolerant normalization path (`bip39.rs:111-154`), and a well-understood KDF.

- **Adversarial stress.** *"Paper is fragile and coercible. The whole scheme collapses to a 12-word string a human must protect; an adversary who photographs the paper, or coerces the witness to recite it, owns everything — including past archives that forward secrecy was supposed to protect."* **Honest concession with scoping.** This is true and is the deliberate trust boundary: the design protects against *device* compromise, not against compromise of the *human + paper*. The motto is "a *seizure* exposes nothing" — a seizure is of the phone. Once the phrase is disclosed (paper found, or witness coerced), `ArchiveIdentity` re-derives and past archives stored on the relay become readable. The mitigation is operational (the witness controls where/whether the paper exists), and the rebuttal is comparative: every alternative that keeps the device usable after coercion is *strictly worse*, because a TEE-bound key is decryptable on the seized device with the same coerced PIN, with no off-device recovery as compensation. There is also an optional BIP-39 passphrase ("25th word") plumbed through the whole stack (`bip39.rs:167-176`, `identity.rs:198`, all `from_mnemonic` signatures) which, if used, is a second secret the witness need never write down — but it is not currently surfaced as a first-class onboarding step.

- **Assumptions & residual.** Rests on: OS CSPRNG quality (audit confirms all secrets flow from `OsRng`, `01_CRYPTO_RED.md:109`); the witness backing up to paper and protecting it; PBKDF2-2048 being the chosen seed KDF (this is the BIP-39 standard, weak as a *password* KDF but the input here is 128 bits of real entropy, so the iteration count is not the security boundary). It explicitly does **not** claim protection against phrase disclosure.

- **Questions for the future audit.**
  1. Should the BIP-39 passphrase ("25th word") be promoted to a first-class, optional onboarding feature for the high-coercion-risk user — and what is its UX/data-loss trade-off (a forgotten passphrase is an unrecoverable archive)?
  2. Is 2048 PBKDF2 iterations defensible given the input is high-entropy (yes in theory), and does any path ever feed *low-entropy* material into `mnemonic_to_seed` that would make the iteration count load-bearing?

### 2.2 Paper-backup confirmation by re-typing sample words

- **What it is.** During onboarding the phrase is generated and shown, then the confirm screen reads the held buffer, picks 3 random word positions, and checks the user's input against them (`OnBoardMnemonicConfirmFragment.kt:32-56`). The buffer lives only in `MnemonicHolder` as a `ByteArray` (`MnemonicHolder.kt:30-54`), copied defensively and wiped on `clear()` via `SecureWipe` (`MnemonicHolder.kt:62-77`). It is deliberately *not* passed through an Android `Bundle` (which can serialize to disk) — see the holder's own rationale (`MnemonicHolder.kt:25-26`).

- **Affirmative case.** The single biggest data-loss risk in a sovereign-key design is a witness who never actually wrote the phrase down. Forcing them to read three random words back proves the paper exists *before* enrollment commits, with minimal friction. Keeping the phrase as raw UTF-8 bytes (never a Java `String`) means it never enters the immutable, un-wipeable JVM intern pool (`MnemonicHolder.kt:8-16`); the bytes go straight to Rust which wraps them in `Zeroizing` at the earliest point (`ffi/src/lib.rs:368-376`).

- **Why this implementation.** Alternatives: *no confirmation* (rejected — silent data loss); *full 12-word re-entry* (rejected — friction that pushes users to screenshot the phrase, which is far worse for seizure); *`CharArray`* (the pre-6.1.4-B design, rejected because feeding the FFI required `String(mnemonic)`, materializing an un-wipeable Java String — `MnemonicHolder.kt:13-16`). The 3-of-12 spot check is the chosen friction/assurance balance. The confirm screen also has process-death observability (`OnBoardMnemonicConfirmFragment.kt:34-44`) because a lost holder mid-onboarding manifests as "my phrase changed by itself."

- **Adversarial stress.** *"The phrase is on screen during onboarding — a screenshot, a screen-recorder, or shoulder-surfing captures it; and individual words split into a transient `String` (`OnBoardMnemonicConfirmFragment.kt:51`) leak into the GC heap."* **Partial concession.** The transient split is real but the audit's own reasoning applies: individual BIP-39 words are public wordlist entries, not sensitive in isolation; only the full ordered phrase is the secret, and that stays as `ByteArray` (`OnBoardMnemonicConfirmFragment.kt:47-50`). On-screen display is intrinsic to *any* paper-backup scheme — you cannot have the user copy a phrase they cannot see. FLAG_SECURE on the onboarding window (preventing screenshots/screen-record) is the standard mitigation and is worth the auditor confirming is set.

- **Assumptions & residual.** Rests on the user copying accurately (BIP-39 checksum catches most single-word errors at *restore*, but see 2.6 on the production-path checksum gap) and on the onboarding window being screenshot-protected. Does not claim to defend against shoulder-surfing.

- **Questions for the future audit.**
  1. Is `FLAG_SECURE` set on every onboarding fragment that renders the phrase, and on the confirm screen?
  2. The transient `String(mnemonic, ...)` at confirm time (`OnBoardMnemonicConfirmFragment.kt:51`) — is it the only place the full ordered phrase becomes a JVM `String`, and is its GC-bounded lifetime acceptable given it carries the whole secret for the split duration?

### 2.3 Role-split deterministic derivation via per-context HKDF

- **What it is.** From the one 64-byte seed, four independent key trees are derived, each under a distinct, hard-coded HKDF context string so that the same seed yields cryptographically unrelated keys: identity Ed25519 (`b"stream.identity.ed25519.v1"`), encryption X25519 (`b"stream.encryption.x25519.v1"`), ratchet chain-0 (`b"stream.ratchet.chain0.v2"`) — all in `identity.rs:52-54`; provenance (`b"stream.provenance.ed25519.v1"`, `provenance.rs:53`); and the report capability master (`b"stream.report.master.v1"`, then a per-index sub-derivation, `report.rs:68-71`). The X25519 secret reproduces libsodium's `crypto_box_seed_keypair` exactly (`sk = SHA-512(seed)[..32]`, `identity.rs:212-220, 369-372`) to stay wire-compatible with the Android seal side.

- **Affirmative case.** One memorized/paper secret, many keys, zero correlation. A compromise of one derived key (say the provenance key disclosed in a court filing) tells the adversary nothing about the others (HKDF is a PRF; distinct `info` → independent outputs — proven by test `provenance.rs:258-273` asserting the provenance pk ≠ identity pk). Determinism is what makes the phrase *sovereign*: the rescue device reconstructs every key and every report address from the phrase alone, with no on-device state (`report.rs:19-24`). It also serves the relay-blind property: reports are addressed by `report_id = H(ctx ‖ report_pk_n)` where `report_pk_n` derives from the seed, so the relay can store `report_id → report_pk` and never learn the identity (`report.rs:5-24, 105-121`).

- **Why this implementation.** Alternatives: *one key reused for everything* (rejected — cross-protocol attacks, and a single disclosure exposes all roles); *independent random keys per role each separately stored* (rejected — destroys sovereignty: you would have to back up N secrets, and they would have to live on the device or be escrowed). HKDF-from-one-seed gives the best of both: independence *and* single-secret recovery. The context strings are documented as immutable wire constants (`identity.rs:32-36`, `report.rs:67-68`) because changing one orphans every enrolled identity — a correct and explicitly-flagged constraint.

- **Adversarial stress.** *"Everything hangs off one seed. HKDF independence is only computational, and the whole tree shares a single root — there is no key separation at the trust-boundary level, just at the math level. One seed compromise is total."* **Rebuttal (scoped).** Correct that root compromise is total — but that is inherent and intended: the root *is* the sovereign identity, and the design's job is to keep that root off the device (2.1), not to make the device hold N independent un-correlatable secrets (which would defeat recovery). Within the "root is safe" assumption, the per-context separation does real work: it is what lets the provenance/report systems be *offline-* and *relay-verified* without leaking the identity, and it is reinforced by explicit one-byte signature-domain separation (`signature_domain.rs:19-57`) so a signature in one role never validates in another (tested cross-domain reject, `identity.rs:487-509`, `report.rs:444-461`). The separation is therefore defense-in-depth layered on top of the single-root reality, not a claim that the roots are independent.

- **Assumptions & residual.** Rests on HKDF-SHA256 as a PRF and on the context strings never drifting from the Kotlin/server mirrors. Does not claim per-role compromise independence *above* the root.

- **Questions for the future audit.**
  1. Are all HKDF context strings byte-identical across Rust core, Kotlin, the CLI rescue tool, and the server verifier? (A silent drift is an unrecoverable-archive bug, not just a verify failure.)
  2. The X25519 `sk = SHA-512(seed)[..32]` libsodium-shape derivation (`identity.rs:212-220`) — is the clamping handled correctly downstream by both dalek and the Android `crypto_box` so the keypair halves agree across the boundary?

### 2.4 V2 lifecycle asymmetry: one-shot long-term key, on-demand archive key

- **What it is.** Three types with deliberately different lifetimes (`identity.rs:1-36`): `StreamIdentity` is public-only (Ed25519 + X25519 pubs), cheap to clone, safe to store and `Debug`-print (`identity.rs:66-159`). `EnrollmentKit` holds the long-term Ed25519 secret + chain-0 in `LockedSecret`s wrapped in `Option`, and is consumed once — sign the first batch, hand chain-0 to the ratchet, then drop/`wipe`, after which the device *cannot sign with the long-term key again* (`identity.rs:176-322`; consume-once enforced by `take_chain_zero` returning `AlreadyConsumed`, `identity.rs:301-305`, tested `identity.rs:522-542`). `ArchiveIdentity` holds *only* the X25519 secret, re-derived on demand from the phrase for unsealing archived session keys, then dropped (`identity.rs:338-428`).

- **Affirmative case.** This is forward secrecy at the *identity* layer. After enrollment, the long-term signing key is gone from the device — a seizure cannot mint new enrollments or forge the witness's foundational signature, because the key that does so no longer exists in RAM or on disk. Day-to-day signing is delegated to the ephemeral ratchet (whose own forward secrecy means a live extraction forges only the current batch's remaining slots, never the past — `00_DESIGN_CRITIQUE.md:17`). The archive-decryption key is *absent by default* and materialized only for the seconds an archive-mode session needs it (`identity.rs:341-342` "drop this instance as soon as the archive-mode session ends"). So in steady state the device holds *no* key capable of decrypting a past archive — it must re-derive X25519 from the phrase to do so.

- **Why this implementation.** Alternatives: *keep the long-term key live for re-signing* (rejected — turns the phone into the vault; a seizure forges identity). *Keep X25519 resident for fast archive access* (rejected — a resident decryption key is exactly what a seizure wants; the cost of re-deriving on demand is one PBKDF2 + HKDF, paid only when the witness deliberately enters archive mode). The `Option<LockedSecret>` + type-level consume-once is chosen over a runtime boolean flag so the "already consumed" state is unrepresentable-as-usable, enforced across the FFI by wrapping in `Mutex<Option<_>>` (`ffi/src/lib.rs:338-340`, doc at `ffi/src/lib.rs:18-22`).

- **Adversarial stress.** *"`EnrollmentKit::sign_once` is documented as multi-call ('Kotlin allows multiple calls before close', `identity.rs:261-263`) — the one-shot promise is enforced only by the kit dropping 'shortly after enroll', not by the type. If the kit is held longer than intended, the long-term key is live and signs arbitrary messages."* **Concession, scoped to a narrow window.** True: `sign_once` is not literally once; only `take_chain_zero` is type-enforced single-shot (`identity.rs:301-305`). The mitigation is structural, not type-level: the live Kotlin enrollment path constructs the kit, signs `batch_0`, takes chain-0, and `wipe()`s in a `finally` (`StreamUploadManager.kt:153-258`, `kit.wipe(); kit.destroy()` at `256-257`). So the actual window where the long-term key is live is the body of `enrollFromMnemonic`. The honest residual: an auditor must verify no code path keeps an `EnrollmentKit` reference past enrollment. The 0x06 `ProvenanceKeyAttestation` domain (`signature_domain.rs:26, 49-51`) deliberately exploits this same narrow window — it is the *only* moment the long-term key can vouch for the provenance key — which is a coherent use of the window, not a leak.

- **Assumptions & residual.** Rests on the caller dropping the kit promptly (verified in the live path) and on `LockedSecret`/`Mutex` drop semantics under `panic = "unwind"` (`01_CRYPTO_RED.md:113`) so a panic mid-enrollment still runs the wipe. Does not claim `sign_once` is cryptographically single-use.

- **Questions for the future audit.**
  1. Trace every construction site of `EnrollmentKit` (Rust + the FFI `Mutex<Option>`): is there any path — error, retry (`EnrollmentRetryWorker`), config-change — where the kit outlives `enrollFromMnemonic` and the long-term key stays live?
  2. Is the long-term Ed25519 secret ever persisted *anywhere* (it should not be — only `StreamIdentity` pubs are saved, `StreamUploadManager.kt:172`)? Confirm no debug/backup path serializes it.

### 2.5 PIN-sealing with Argon2id + XChaCha20-Poly1305

- **What it is.** The few secrets that must survive a lock/unlock cycle on disk (the ratchet blob, the provenance seed, the report master) are sealed under the user's PIN: Argon2id (m = 256 MiB, t = 4, p = 1, 32-byte output) derives a key (`pin_store.rs:341-361`), then XChaCha20-Poly1305 with a per-seal random salt+nonce and a fixed domain-separating AAD (`b"frappuccino-v2-pin-store-v1"`) seals the plaintext (`pin_store.rs:94-139`). A wrong PIN and a tampered blob are *deliberately indistinguishable* (both → `WrongPin`, `pin_store.rs:179-189`, tested `pin_store.rs:397-406`); the Kotlin `PinAttemptTracker` owns lockout policy (`pin_store.rs:27-33`).

- **Affirmative case.** Argon2id at 256 MiB is tuned for ~1.2 s on the target hardware (Solana Seeker / Snapdragon 8+ Gen 1, `pin_store.rs:5-8`), which makes offline brute-force of a 6-digit PIN economically painful even with GPUs (the 256 MiB memory-hardness is the point — it defeats the massively-parallel cracking a state lab would throw at a stolen sealed blob). The fixed AAD binds every blob to this app/version so a blob cannot be replayed into a different context. The wrong-PIN/tampered ambiguity denies an attacker an oracle.

- **Why this implementation.** Alternatives: *PBKDF2/scrypt* (rejected — Argon2id is the modern memory-hard standard and resists GPU/ASIC better than scrypt at equal tuning); *AES-GCM* (XChaCha20-Poly1305 chosen for its 24-byte random nonce, which makes nonce reuse a non-issue under random generation — `pin_store.rs:95-98` — versus AES-GCM's 96-bit nonce where random generation is riskier at volume; also constant-time in software without AES-NI dependence). The `seal_with_key`/`open_with_key`/`open_extended` fast-reseal trio (`pin_store.rs:203-331`) exists so the ~1.2 s Argon2id is paid *once per unlock*, not once per ratchet signature (the ratchet blob is rewritten after every signature) — a real usability requirement for screen-off recording.

- **Adversarial stress.** *"A 6-digit PIN has only ~20 bits of entropy. Argon2id slows each guess to 1.2 s, but a state adversary who seizes the sealed blob and the device can also coerce the PIN, or run 10^6 guesses at 1.2 s each on a cluster (~14 device-days, trivially parallelized across the blob copies). The KDF buys time, not secrecy."* **Concession with the correct framing.** True — a 6-digit PIN is not the security boundary against a determined state lab; it is a *speed bump* and a *lockout trigger*. The actual forward-secrecy guarantee does not rest on the PIN: the ratchet's past slots are wiped (`01_CRYPTO_RED.md:99`), the X25519 archive key is re-derivable only from the *phrase* (not the PIN), and chunks are sealed to X25519 and uploaded+secure-deleted. So cracking the PIN yields the *current* ratchet state and the seed-derived provenance/report secrets — i.e., the ability to impersonate the witness's *future* uploads and to enumerate reports — **but not the plaintext of past archives** (those need the phrase, §2.1/§2.4). The PIN's job is to (a) gate quick unlock, (b) feed the lockout tracker, and (c) raise the cost of a snatch-and-grab. The auditor should treat "PIN cracked" as in-model and check that nothing past-exposing falls out of it.

- **Assumptions & residual.** Rests on Argon2id parameters actually achieving memory-hardness on real devices (256 MiB is large; low-RAM devices may OOM — `DerivationFailed` surfaces this, `pin_store.rs:91-93`), on the OS CSPRNG for salt/nonce, and on the Kotlin lockout tracker being enforced (out of scope for the Rust core by design). Does not claim PIN secrecy against coercion or a memory-cluster.

- **Questions for the future audit.**
  1. Is the Kotlin `PinAttemptTracker` lockout (the *real* defense against PIN brute-force) tamper-resistant — can resetting `SharedPreferences` or reinstalling reset the attempt counter and the sealed blobs survive? If so the lockout is bypassable and only the 1.2 s/guess remains.
  2. On a low-RAM device, what happens when 256 MiB Argon2id OOMs — does the app degrade to a weaker KDF (it must not) or fail closed?
  3. Could the PIN be combined with a device-bound secret (TEE-sealed salt) so an *offline* blob (relay backup, exfiltrated `filesDir`) cannot be cracked without the device — without reintroducing the "TEE owns the key" anti-pattern?

### 2.6 `mlock`'d `LockedSecret` + `Zeroizing` + scoped accessor

- **What it is.** Two confinement wrappers (`secret.rs:1-23`): `SecretBytes` (heap, zero-on-drop, redacted `Debug`) for short-lived material, and `LockedSecret` (heap, **`mlock`'d** so it never swaps to disk, zero-then-`munlock` on drop, redacted `Debug`) for longer-lived keys (`secret.rs:104-209`). Both expose bytes *only* through a `with_bytes(f)` closure whose reference cannot escape (`secret.rs:76-84, 161-169`) — preventing accidental `.to_vec()` copies into longer-lived buffers. `mlock` failure is fail-closed (`DerivationFailed`, not silent degradation, `secret.rs:140-145`). `write_and_wipe_source` moves a stack secret into the locked page and wipes the source in one call (`secret.rs:177-188`, tested `secret.rs:257-264`).

- **Affirmative case.** `mlock` closes the swap-to-disk leak: a key that pages out could land in a swapfile that survives reboot and is trivially extracted from a seized device. The scoped accessor is *misuse-resistance by construction* — the type system makes the dangerous operation (copying the secret out) require deliberate effort, so the easy path is the safe path. Redacted `Debug` (tested for every secret type, e.g. `secret.rs:236-240, 273-277`, `bip39.rs:388-398`) means a stray `log.d("$secret")` or a panic message never spills key bytes into logcat — which on Android is readable by other processes and forensic tools.

- **Why this implementation.** Alternatives: *raw `[u8; N]`* (rejected — no wipe, no mlock, derivable `Debug` leaks; `Rust_guidelines.md` mandates typed wrappers, cited `bip39.rs:45-46`); *exposing `&[u8]` getters* (rejected — the reference outlives the intended scope and invites `.to_vec()`); *a single wrapper* (rejected — `mlock` has a per-page rlimit cost not worth paying for every ephemeral 32-byte buffer, hence the `SecretBytes`/`LockedSecret` split). The `Box<[u8]>` backing never reallocates, so the locked region stays at a stable address for its lifetime (`secret.rs:110-116`).

- **Adversarial stress.** *"`mlock` only stops swap. It does nothing against `/proc/pid/mem` on a rooted device, against a cold-boot/DMA attack, or against the Rust allocator handing the freed slot to another allocation before the OS zeroes the page. And the `with_bytes` discipline is voluntary — `write_and_wipe_source` and the derivation paths still copy the secret onto the stack (`identity.rs:285-291` holds the Ed25519 seed in a `Zeroizing` stack buffer precisely because of this)."* **Rebuttal + concession.** The stack-copy concern is *handled*, not hand-waved: every transient derived secret is `Zeroizing`-wrapped so its drop runs a volatile wipe on every path including unwind (`identity.rs:285-287`, `report.rs:213-216`, `bip39.rs:206, 285`), and the zeroize-audit proves these wipes are not dead-store-eliminated (§2.8). The `/proc/pid/mem` and live-RAM concerns are *conceded* and are exactly the accepted residual R-D-1 (§2.10): `mlock` is not claimed to defend a live, unlocked, rooted device — it defends the *swap* and *post-drop* surfaces, which it does.

- **Assumptions & residual.** Rests on the OS honoring `mlock`/`VirtualLock`, on the `zeroize` crate's volatile semantics, and on developers using `with_bytes` rather than reaching around it. Does not claim protection of live RAM on a rooted device.

- **Scope caveat (2026-06-30 cross-audit) — the live ratchet batch is `Zeroize`-on-drop but NOT `mlock`'d.** `EphemeralRatchet.private_keys` (`[[u8; 64]; 50]`, the largest resident secret while recording) and `next_chain_key` are plain arrays wiped on drop (`ratchet.rs:283, 345, 493`), **not** `LockedSecret` — unlike the long-term keys (`ProvenanceSigner`, `ReportKeyring`). So "longer-lived keys use `LockedSecret`" above is **not universal**: the ratchet batch *can* page to swap. **Bounded:** the **Android target has no disk swapfile** (zram = compressed RAM, not disk), so the swap-to-disk surface `mlock` defends is largely moot on-device; the live-RAM/rooted exposure is the already-accepted R-D-1 either way. **Decision (kept `Zeroize`-only):** wrapping the hot-path batch in `LockedSecret` buys ~nothing on zram and would touch the ratchet's most sensitive struct ("ne pas casser le ratchet"). This resolves the R-D-1 register's note on §2.6 over-breadth (`ACCEPTED_RESIDUALS_2026-06-28.md`).

- **Questions for the future audit.**
  1. Grep for any `with_bytes` usage that copies the slice out (`.to_vec()`, `.to_owned()`, `copy_from_slice` into a non-`Zeroizing`/non-`LockedSecret` target) — these are the spots where the confinement is voluntarily broken; confirm each lands in a `Zeroizing`/`LockedSecret`.
  2. Does `mlock` actually succeed on the target devices' default `RLIMIT_MEMLOCK`, or does it routinely fail and (correctly) fail-closed — i.e., is fail-closed ever blocking a legitimate unlock in the field?

### 2.7 No-export design (Lot 4b): the master key never crosses the FFI

- **What it is.** The Argon2id-derived master key — which decrypts *every* PIN-sealed blob — is never returned to Kotlin. It lives in a process-global `Zeroizing` static inside Rust (`PIN_SESSION`, `ffi/src/lib.rs:1375-1380`), accessed only through a closure that never lets the reference escape (`with_pin_session`, `ffi/src/lib.rs:1417-1419`). All open/seal operations happen *entirely in-crate*: `pin_session_open_ratchet` runs Argon2id once, stashes the key, and deserializes the ratchet without the 50-key plaintext crossing the boundary (`ffi/src/lib.rs:1464-1477`); `reseal_session_blob`, `seal_with_session`, `pin_session_open_provenance_signer`, `pin_session_open_report_keyring` all consume the held key in-crate (`ffi/src/lib.rs:614-626, 973-982, 1487-1527`). The raw `pin_store_open_extended`/`*_with_key` exports that *did* return the key were retired (`ffi/src/lib.rs:263-271`). The holder is cleared on lock/panic but, critically, **never on a 401** — because unlike the upload bearer the key is not re-derivable without the PIN, so clearing it mid-recording would strand chunks (`ffi/src/lib.rs:1354-1364, 1421-1426`).

- **Affirmative case.** This is the strongest single hardening in the domain. Before Lot 4b, the master key round-tripped through a JVM `ByteArray` and a UniFFI `RustBuffer`, neither of which is zeroized at free (the audit's R-CR-1/R-CR-3, `01_CRYPTO_RED.md:41-55, 76-87`). By keeping the key Rust-side behind an opaque handle, the *entire JVM heap* — the part of memory most exposed to heap-dump tooling and the least controllable for wiping — never contains the master key, the 50-key ratchet plaintext, the provenance seed, or the report master. The pattern mirrors the proven `strm_decrypt_to_file` (plaintext stays on disk/Rust, never the JVM heap, `ffi/src/lib.rs:855-896`) and the upload-bearer holder.

- **Why this implementation.** Alternatives: *keep returning the key and wipe it in Kotlin* (the pre-Lot-4b design, rejected — `SecureWipe` is JIT-resistant on the JVM but cannot wipe the intermediate `RustBuffer`, and an immutable `String`/un-wipeable buffer window remains, `01_CRYPTO_RED.md:51-53`); *a fresh Argon2id per operation* (rejected — 1.2 s × every-signature is unusable for screen-off recording). A process-global static (vs threading a handle object) is chosen so the holder lifetime is decoupled from any single Kotlin object and the clear path (lock/panic) can always reach it, matching the upload-bearer holder it parallels.

- **Adversarial stress.** *"A process-global mutable static holding the master key for the whole unlocked session is still a resident secret — on a live, unlocked, rooted device a `/proc/pid/mem` dump finds it in the Rust heap just as easily as the JVM heap. You moved the secret, you did not remove it; and a global static is a single well-known target."* **Rebuttal (scoping the win precisely).** Correct that the secret is still resident while unlocked — but that is the *irreducible* "unlocked session" residual (R-D-1), not the thing no-export claimed to fix. What no-export *does* fix is concrete and verified: (a) it removes the secret from the JVM heap entirely (the heap most exposed to standard Android dump tooling), (b) it eliminates the un-wipeable `RustBuffer`/`ByteArray` *copies* that lingered after use even post-`lock()`, and (c) the held key is `Zeroizing`, so `lock()`/`panicWipe()` deterministically wipe it (`ffi/src/lib.rs:1376, 1424-1426`; `StreamUploadManager.kt:352`). The post-`lock()` surface — a device seized *locked*, or dumped after backgrounding — is the realistic seizure scenario, and there no-export makes the heap clean. The live-unlocked-rooted dump is the conceded R-D-1.

- **Assumptions & residual.** Rests on the `Zeroizing` static actually wiping (covered by the zeroize discipline; note the *static* itself is `Option<PinSessionState>` set to `None`, which drops and wipes the inner `Zeroizing` key, `ffi/src/lib.rs:1405-1408, 1424-1425`), and on every Kotlin secret-handling path having migrated off the retired exports (the audit was written *before* the P6 retirement, so R-CR-1/R-CR-3's `pin_store_open_extended`/`ratchetDerivedKey` citations are now stale — see §3). Does not claim to protect a live unlocked session.

- **Questions for the future audit.**
  1. Confirm the retirement is *complete*: no Kotlin caller still invokes a key-returning export, and the only surviving `pin_store_open`/`pin_store_seal` are the diff-fuzz-only paths with zero prod callers (`ffi/src/lib.rs:268-271`).
  2. The holder is `static Mutex<Option<PinSessionState>>` with poison-recovery (`ffi/src/lib.rs:1382-1389`) — under a panic that poisons the mutex mid-recording, does the recover-into-inner path ever leave a stale key alive past a `lock()` it should have cleared?
  3. Setting the static to `None` drops the `Zeroizing<[u8;32]>` — is there any reallocation/move of `PinSessionState` (e.g. on replace) that copies the old key bytes to a new address *before* the old is wiped (`pin_session_store` replaces via `*guard = Some(...)`, `ffi/src/lib.rs:1405`)?

### 2.8 Compiler-verified zeroize (no dead-store elimination)

- **What it is.** The ratchet's secret wipe (50 × 64-byte private keys + chain key) is proven, at the LLVM-IR level, to survive optimization — it lowers to `store volatile` / out-of-line `zeroize::Zeroize` calls, never a DSE-able plain memset (`docs/ZEROIZE_AUDIT_RATCHET.md:32-52`). An executable tripwire (`crypto-rs/core/audit/assert_zeroize_not_dse.sh`) re-checks the invariant at both the shipping `opt-level=s` and the DSE-aggressive `opt-level=2`, and was validated to *fail* on a regressed `*sk = [0u8;N]` (`ZEROIZE_AUDIT_RATCHET.md:94-99`).

- **Affirmative case.** "We zeroize on drop" is a claim most projects cannot back up, because a compiler is free to delete a store whose result is never read — and a `Drop` body's wipe is *by definition* never read afterward (`ZEROIZE_AUDIT_RATCHET.md:6-13`). The only honest proof is at the IR level, and this domain ships one plus a regression guard. All six sensitive object types pass (`ZEROIZE_AUDIT_RATCHET.md:54-56`).

- **Why this implementation.** Alternatives: *trust the `zeroize` crate's contract* (the crate is correct, but the audit confirms the *shipping profile* `opt-level=s` behaves differently from O2 — the wipe goes out-of-line rather than inlining, which a naive `grep 'store volatile'` would false-fail on, `ZEROIZE_AUDIT_RATCHET.md:58-72`; so trusting blindly would have shipped a guard that lies). The tripwire encodes the *profile-robust* invariant instead.

- **Adversarial stress.** *"The evidence is per-crate, pre-LTO IR; the final LTO'd binary is not separately dumped (`ZEROIZE_AUDIT_RATCHET.md:115-116`). And it proves only that the wipe *executes*, not that no *other* copy of the secret (register spill, an earlier stack temp) survives."* **Concession, already documented.** Both limits are stated in the audit itself (`ZEROIZE_AUDIT_RATCHET.md:114-122`): LTO preserving volatile is a contract, not a separate dump; and stack/register copies are a separate source-level concern covered by the `Zeroizing` temporaries and the clean source pass. This is the honest scope of a regression tripwire, not a whole-binary information-flow proof.

- **Assumptions & residual.** Rests on the LLVM volatile contract surviving LTO and on the guard being run in CI/pre-release (note: the repo has no remote and CI is dormant — the guard is a manual gate). Does not claim register-spill or whole-binary coverage.

- **Questions for the future audit.**
  1. Is the tripwire actually run before each release build, given dormant CI? A guard that is never executed is documentation, not enforcement.
  2. Extend the same IR check to the *other* five sensitive types under `opt-level=s` (the audit confirms all six pass, but the *guard* targets `zeroize_secrets`) — are `LockedSecret::drop`, `Seed`, `EnrollmentKit` equally guarded against a future regression?

### 2.9 Auto-lock: wipe the ratchet at idle, never during recording/drain

- **What it is.** A `ProcessLifecycleOwner` observer (`V2LockTimeoutController.kt:51-234`) runs two independent background timers: the legacy JWT-clear (`fire()`, `:141-169`) and the Phase-6 ratchet inactivity auto-lock (`fireRatchetLock()`, `:201-234`). On idle, `fireRatchetLock` calls `StreamUploadManager.lock()` (`:232`), wiping the ratchet, provenance signer, report keyring, and the PIN-session key (`StreamUploadManager.kt:332-354`), forcing a re-PIN on return. Critically, it **defers** if a recording is live, tearing down, still encrypting, or has chunks pending drain (`:212-231`) — and the `backgrounded` guard stops a deferred fire from locking after the user returns (`:201, 222-223`).

- **Affirmative case.** This shrinks the "unlocked window" — the one window where the resident secrets (§2.7, R-D-1) are exposed — to roughly the user's actual active+idle-timeout time, instead of "until reboot." A phone left on a table, backgrounded, auto-locks and wipes the ratchet from RAM, so a *later* seizure finds a locked device with a clean secret surface. It directly serves "a seizure exposes nothing" by making the common seizure case (device not in active use) a locked-device case.

- **Why this implementation.** Alternatives: *wipe on every background* (rejected — catastrophic for screen-off witness recording, the app's core use case; wiping the ratchet mid-session crashes chunk encryption and strands footage — the exact data-loss class that drove the 1.14/3.38-D gates, `V2LockTimeoutController.kt:120-139`). *Never auto-lock* (rejected — leaves the secret resident indefinitely). The two-timer split keeps the field-tuned JWT timing (default 0) untouched while adding a *separate*, configurable ratchet timer (`:65-70, 101-106`). The drain-safe deferral is the hard-won correctness: it mirrors the JWT clear's gates because `report_master` is *not* re-derivable in the background, so a mid-drain wipe loses pending chunks (`:202-211`).

- **Adversarial stress.** *"The deferral logic is a loophole: as long as a recording or a drain is in flight, the ratchet is *never* wiped, no matter how long. A witness who starts a long screen-off recording and is then seized has a fully-unlocked device with all secrets resident — auto-lock provides exactly zero protection in the highest-stakes scenario (active recording during a raid)."* **Concession — this is the deliberate trade-off.** During active recording the device *must* hold live secrets to encrypt and upload chunks; there is no way to both record and hold no key. The design chooses "never lose the witness's footage mid-session" over "lock during recording," because losing footage is a guaranteed harm while seizure-during-recording is a contingent one — and even then, forward secrecy means the seizure exposes only the *current* session's slots, never past archives (`00_DESIGN_CRITIQUE.md:17`). The witness retains the explicit `panicWipe()` (`StreamUploadManager.kt:375`) and the shake-to-lock for the raid case. Auto-lock is for the *idle* device, and it is honestly scoped to that.

- **Assumptions & residual.** Rests on the lifecycle callbacks firing reliably (Android can be hostile to background timers; doze/OEM killers may delay the fire), on the drain-pending count being accurate (`getPendingCount` + `encryptionsInFlight`, `:212-220`), and on the user configuring a non-disabled timeout (default and bounds in `Preferences.getRatchetAutoLockMs`). Does not claim protection during active recording.

- **Questions for the future audit.**
  1. Can the deferral livelock — a pathological "always something pending" state (e.g. a chunk perpetually failing to drain) that defers the ratchet lock forever, defeating auto-lock entirely? What bounds the reschedule (`:225-227` reschedules unconditionally while busy)?
  2. Are the lifecycle `ON_PAUSE`/`ON_RESUME` callbacks robust to Android's background-execution limits and OEM process management, or can the auto-lock silently never fire on some devices?
  3. The auto-lock wipes `reportKeyring` at `lock()` but the code notes a known drain-asymmetry residual (`StreamUploadManager.kt:340-347`) — is the lock-vs-drain race fully closed for the *explicit* `lock()` (the auto-lock path defers, but does manual lock during a drain strand chunks)?

### 2.10 Accepted residuals: R-D-1 (live heap-dump) and R-C-2

- **What it is.** The design explicitly accepts that a **live, unlocked, rooted** device can be heap-dumped to recover *current-session* secrets (R-D-1), and the related device-dependent residuals (R-C-2; restore/backup, root). The audit's R-CR-1 (FFI `.to_vec()` copies of secrets surviving in the Rust heap) and R-CR-3 (Argon2id master in a JVM `ByteArray`) were both classified into this same envelope (`01_CRYPTO_RED.md:52-53, 84-85`), and R-CR-1's specific window was subsequently *closed* by the Lot 4b no-export migration (§2.7).

- **Affirmative case (why accepting is correct).** The motto is about *seizure*, and a seizure is of a device the witness is not actively using — i.e., locked or backgrounded, where the secret surface is clean (§2.7, §2.9). A *live unlocked rooted* device is a fundamentally different, much narrower threat: the adversary already has root on a running, unlocked phone, in which case essentially *no* userspace defense survives (they can read any process memory, hook the crypto, log the PIN entry). Crucially, even that worst case does **not** expose past archives: forward secrecy of the ratchet (`01_CRYPTO_RED.md:99`), chunks already sealed-to-X25519 + uploaded + secure-deleted, and the X25519 archive key being re-derivable only from the *phrase* (absent by default, §2.4) mean the live dump yields only the current session, never the rushes already gone (`01_CRYPTO_RED.md:53, 85`; `00_DESIGN_CRITIQUE.md:17`). Drawing the line here is the honest, defensible boundary.

- **Why this implementation (why not chase it further).** Closing R-D-1 fully would require defeating root — which userspace cannot do. The design instead invests where it pays: removing secrets from the JVM heap (§2.7), shrinking the unlocked window (§2.9), proving the wipes are real (§2.8), and keeping the *past* unreachable regardless (forward secrecy). The residual is the small, intrinsic "unlocked session" surface, and it is documented rather than hidden.

- **Adversarial stress.** *"'We accept it' can mask a creeping surface. R-CR-1 showed secret *copies* outliving their intended scope in the Rust heap — that is not 'intrinsic to unlocked session,' it is a wipe bug, and it was found by an auditor, not by the design. How many more such copies exist under the 'accepted residual' umbrella?"* **Rebuttal + the right humility.** Fair — and the project's response was exactly right: R-CR-1 was *fixed* (no-export, §2.7), not waved away, even though it was already inside the accepted envelope. That is the test of an honest residual: the intrinsic part (root reads live RAM) is accepted; the *avoidable* part (gratuitous copies, JVM-heap residency) is closed when found. The standing question for the next audit is precisely the one the stress raises — enumerate, don't assume.

- **Assumptions & residual.** Rests on forward secrecy holding (separately proven: Tamarin/TLA+/Kani in the ratchet domain) and on the past genuinely being unreachable from current-session secrets. Explicitly does **not** claim to defend a live, unlocked, rooted device's *current* session.

- **Questions for the future audit.**
  1. Systematic sweep: every place a secret leaves a `LockedSecret`/`Zeroizing` (every `with_bytes`/`with_*_bytes`/`.to_vec()`/`copy_from_slice`) — does the destination get wiped, or is it a fresh R-CR-1-class copy hiding under "accepted"?
  2. Is there a *negative* proof the past is unreachable — i.e., confirm no current-session secret (PIN-session key, ratchet, report master, provenance seed) can decrypt or re-address an *already-uploaded* archive chunk? (The claim is X25519-from-phrase-only; verify nothing else suffices.)
  3. The restore/backup vector behind R-C-2 — does Android auto-backup (`allowBackup`) or any cloud sync ever capture `filesDir` PIN-sealed blobs, turning an offline-blob crack (§2.5) into a remote one?

## 3. Domain coherence & tensions

The choices form one coherent thesis: **the phone holds the minimum, holds it briefly, and holds nothing that exposes the past.** Sovereignty (§2.1) + role-split derivation (§2.3) means one paper secret regenerates everything, so the device can afford to hold almost nothing long-term. Lifecycle asymmetry (§2.4) removes the long-term signing key after enrollment and keeps the archive key absent by default. PIN-sealing (§2.5) + `mlock`/`Zeroizing` (§2.6) + no-export (§2.7) protect the few secrets that *must* persist or stay resident, and the zeroize audit (§2.8) proves the wipes are real. Auto-lock (§2.9) shrinks the resident window. The accepted residuals (§2.10) are the honest boundary, and forward secrecy is the backstop that makes even the worst in-model case (live unlocked root) past-safe.

Internal tensions worth flagging for the next audit:

- **Usability vs. resident-secret window.** The single biggest tension. Fast-reseal (§2.5/§2.7) and the auto-lock deferral (§2.9) both exist because screen-off recording *requires* live secrets for the whole session. Every "hold the key longer" decision trades the R-D-1 surface for not-losing-footage. The design consistently chooses footage, justified by forward secrecy — but the auditor should pressure-test whether the *deferral* can be stretched indefinitely (livelock) and whether the unlocked window is as short as claimed.

- **One root vs. compromise blast radius.** §2.3's HKDF tree is elegant for recovery but means root compromise (phrase disclosure, §2.1) is total. The design accepts this as the inherent cost of sovereignty; the optional passphrase is the only lever, and it is under-surfaced.

- **Type-level vs. structural enforcement.** `take_chain_zero` is type-enforced single-shot, but `sign_once` (§2.4) is only structurally one-shot (the kit drops promptly). The one-shot long-term-key promise is therefore a *convention verified in the live path*, not a type invariant — a gap an auditor should close by tracing kit lifetimes.

- **Documentation drift (an audit-integrity hazard, not a runtime bug).** The 2026-06-26 audit's R-CR-1/R-CR-3 cite `pin_store_open_extended` and a `ratchetDerivedKey` JVM `ByteArray` that the *later* Lot 4b P6 no-export migration retired (`ffi/src/lib.rs:263-271`). The findings are stale, not wrong-at-the-time. The next audit must read the code at HEAD, not the prior findings list — the no-export holder (§2.7) is the current reality, and several "accepted residual" citations point at code that no longer exists.

## 4. Top-3 questions this domain hands to the future adversarial audit

1. **Is the no-export migration actually complete and leak-free at HEAD?** Confirm no Kotlin path still calls a key-returning FFI export; that the surviving `pin_store_open`/`seal` are diff-fuzz-only with zero prod callers (`ffi/src/lib.rs:268-271`); and that the process-global `PIN_SESSION` static never copies the master key to a new address before wiping the old, and is always cleared by `lock()`/`panicWipe()` (`ffi/src/lib.rs:1375-1426`, `StreamUploadManager.kt:352`). This is the strongest hardening in the domain — verify it holds.

2. **Can the auto-lock be defeated by the recording/drain deferral, and is the resident-secret window as short as claimed?** Probe for a livelock where "something always pending" defers the ratchet wipe forever (`V2LockTimeoutController.kt:212-231`), and confirm the lifecycle timers actually fire under Android doze / OEM process-killing. If auto-lock silently never fires, the R-D-1 unlocked window is effectively "until reboot," not "until idle timeout."

3. **Is the boundary between "accepted residual" and "avoidable copy" being policed?** R-CR-1 proved secret copies can outlive their scope under the "live-heap-dump accepted" umbrella and was rightly fixed. Do a systematic sweep of every `with_bytes`/`with_*_bytes`/`.to_vec()`/`copy_from_slice` exit from a `LockedSecret`/`Zeroizing` (`secret.rs`, `identity.rs`, `report.rs`, `provenance.rs`, `ffi/src/lib.rs`) and confirm each destination is itself wiped — and produce the *negative* proof that no current-session secret can expose an already-uploaded past archive (the load-bearing forward-secrecy claim).
